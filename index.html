Eight Journeys Through the Parable of the Prodigal Son

A ruined son walks home rehearsing a plan to earn his way back as a hired servant. His father welcomes a son.

Through eight unhurried journeys and three intimate letters, Miary Andria explores the overlooked depths of a familiar parable, inviting us to reconsider the ways we wander, return, and try to earn our place.

  • The request to become a servant that never appears in the son’s homecoming speech
  • The father’s actions, and the words he leaves unspoken
  • Letters to the parent carrying blame, the young heart eyeing the door, and the soul in a hurry

THE RETURN · Luke 15:14–20

Was it repentance, or just hunger?

A short reflection inspired by the book’s themes

The son’s turning point begins with an ordinary, uncomfortable need: he is hungry. He remembers that his father’s hired servants have food to spare. Before he speaks of his wrongdoing, he thinks about survival.

Does that make his return meaningless? The story invites us to sit with a more searching question. Must our motives be perfectly untangled before we take a step toward home? Hunger may start the journey, even when the traveler does not yet understand everything he needs.

The father sees him while he is still far away. The welcome does not wait for a polished explanation. This is an invitation to examine our motives honestly without making perfect motives a condition for beginning.

PUT IT INTO PRACTICE

Name one need that has turned your attention toward God. Bring it honestly, without dressing it up.

GRACE & BELONGING · Luke 15:18–24

The position was never open.

A short reflection inspired by the book’s themes

Before leaving the distant country, the son rehearses a speech. He will confess his sin, acknowledge his unworthiness, and ask to become a hired servant. It is a plan for survival, but also a plan to negotiate his place.

When he reaches his father, the speech as Luke records it does not include that final request. Instead, the father calls for a robe, a ring, sandals, and a celebration. The son arrives with an employment proposal. The father speaks of his son being found.

This is the question at the heart of The Father Is Not Hiring: where have we confused belonging with wages? Service can grow from love. It need not become an application for the love we long to receive.

PUT IT INTO PRACTICE

Notice one moment when you try to earn God’s welcome. What would the same act look like if it began with gratitude?

THE FATHER · Luke 15:20–24

The embrace before the explanation.

A short reflection inspired by the book’s themes

We often follow the son’s journey so closely that we hurry past the father’s movement. He sees. He feels compassion. He runs. He embraces. He kisses. All of this happens before the son’s recorded confession.

The sequence matters. The father is not portrayed as a distant evaluator waiting to score a speech. His actions make the welcome visible before the son has explained himself. In this reunion, the father’s recorded words are directed to the servants as he prepares the celebration.

This does not erase the son’s wrongdoing. It asks us to consider how compassion can meet someone in the truth of their failure. A welcome can open the way for an honest conversation.

PUT IT INTO PRACTICE

Think of a strained relationship. What small, wise act of compassion could make room for honesty?

THE OTHER SON · Luke 15:25–32

So close to home. Still outside.

A short reflection inspired by the book’s themes

The older son is near the house when he hears the music. When he learns what has happened, he refuses to go in. His distance from the father is suddenly visible, even though he has never left the family’s land.

His protest describes years of service and obedience. Yet he feels overlooked. The father comes out to him too, addressing him as a son and inviting him to see the returned wanderer as his brother.

The parable leaves the invitation open. We are not told whether the older son enters. That unfinished ending turns the question toward us: can we rejoice in a welcome we did not control, for someone we believe has not earned it?

PUT IT INTO PRACTICE

Where has comparison made someone else’s joy feel like your loss? Pray for the freedom to celebrate their good.
